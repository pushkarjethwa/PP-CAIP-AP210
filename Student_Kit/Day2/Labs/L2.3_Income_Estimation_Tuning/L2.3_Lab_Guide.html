Objective & scenario alignment

Horizon Gulf Bank (HGB) is a fictional UAE retail bank with 250,000 customers. Its Board approved Project MIZAN — an AI decisioning platform covering credit, fraud, collateral valuation, cash logistics, customer retention and document automation. You are a junior ML practitioner in the MIZAN squad.

UAE affordability rules cap a borrower's debt-burden ratio at 50% of income. For customers whose salary is transferred to HGB the income is verified; for the other ~45% Credit relies on self-declared figures and paper certificates. Credit Risk asks MIZAN for an income estimation model trained on verified customers and applied to the rest — with an honest error estimate and a conservative figure that can be used in affordability checks.

Progressive architecture: uses L1.1's clean master and L1.3's transaction features; income estimates can enrich credit decisions (Day 3) and the capstone (Day 5).

By the end of this lab you will be able to

  • Train linear regression by mini-batch gradient descent and see learning rate, batch size and epochs at work
  • Beat a domain baseline with a regularised pipeline
  • Compare GridSearchCV, RandomizedSearchCV and HalvingRandomSearchCV on quality and compute cost
  • Quantify tuning optimism with nested cross-validation
  • Analyse errors by segment, spot selection bias, and produce a conservative (P20) income

1Assemble the data — and notice who is missing 7 min

Why this matters

The model learns from customers with verified salaries and is applied to customers without them. If those two groups differ (they do: self-employed people rarely transfer salary), the model is being asked to extrapolate. Spotting this selection bias before modelling is part of the job.

Your tasks

  1. Merge the customer master with the selected transaction features; log-transform balance and card spend.
  2. Split into verified (salary transfer, salary present) and unverified (no transfer). Target y = log(salary).
  3. Compare the employment_type mix of the two groups side by side.
Checkpoint — you should see

≈ 147k verified vs ≈ 97k unverified customers. Self-employed are ≈ 3% of verified but ≈ 27% of unverified — a strong selection bias.

Think like a practitioner

The model will be least reliable for exactly the group Credit cares most about. What would you tell the Credit Risk head?

2Gradient descent by hand: learning rate, batch size, epochs 10 min

Why this matters

Every neural network and SGDRegressor learns by iterative optimisation: take a small batch, compute the gradient of the loss, step downhill by learning rate × gradient, repeat for several epochs. Watching the loss curve tells you whether the learning rate is too small (slow), right, or too large (divergence).

Your tasks

  1. Take 5 standardised numeric features (age, tenure, log balance, log card spend, aecb) and y (centred).
  2. Write minibatch_gd(X, y, lr, batch, epochs) returning the MSE after each epoch.
  3. Plot loss curves for learning rates 0.001, 0.01, 0.1 and 1.5 (batch 256, 10 epochs). Compare final weights with LinearRegression.
Checkpoint — you should see

lr 0.001 converges slowly, 0.01–0.1 converge within 1–2 epochs, 1.5 diverges (loss shoots off the chart). GD weights match the closed-form solution to ≈ 2–3 decimals.

Think like a practitioner

With 134k rows and batch 256, how many weight updates happen in one epoch? Why does a mini-batch beat full-batch here?

3Baseline, then a regularised pipeline 8 min

Why this matters

A model is only worth deploying if it beats what Credit does today: median salary for the applicant's employment type and employer category. We hold out a test set first, then compare that rule with a Ridge pipeline on all features. Errors are reported in percent (MAPE on the original scale) because a AED 2,000 miss matters more at AED 8k than at AED 80k.

Your tasks

  1. Split verified 80/20 (random_state=RNG) into train and test.
  2. Baseline: median salary per employment_type × employer_category on train; apply to test.
  3. Pipeline: ColumnTransformer (median-impute + scale numeric, one-hot categorical) + RidgeCV. Report MAPE and R² (log scale) for both.
Checkpoint — you should see

Segment-median rule: MAPE ≈ 51%, R² ≈ 0.12. Ridge pipeline: clearly better — MAPE ≈ 34%, R² ≈ 0.59 on the log scale.

💡 Hint

If base_pred contains NaN, a segment combination exists in test but not train — fill with the overall median.

4Search shoot-out: grid vs random vs successive halving 15 min

Why this matters

A gradient-boosting regressor has many hyperparameters (learning rate, depth, leaf size, L2). Grid search tries every combination — exhaustive but expensive. Random search samples combinations and usually finds an equally good one far faster. Successive halving gives every candidate a small budget and only promotes the best to more data. We compare best CV score and wall-clock time on the same 40k-row training sample.

Your tasks

  1. Pipeline: same preprocessing (ordinal-encode categoricals) + HistGradientBoostingRegressor(max_iter=200, early_stopping=False).
  2. Grid: learning_rate {0.05, 0.1, 0.2} × max_leaf_nodes {15, 31, 63} × min_samples_leaf {20, 100} × l2 {0, 1} = 36 combos, 3-fold.
  3. RandomizedSearchCV with 12 draws from continuous distributions; HalvingRandomSearchCV with 36 candidates, factor 3, using the number of boosting iterations (22 → 66 → 198) as the budget.
  4. Tabulate best CV R², fits performed and seconds for each.
Checkpoint — you should see

Grid and random search tie at CV R² ≈ 0.650 — random needs a third of the fits and ≈ ¼ of the time (≈ 5 s vs ≈ 19 s). Halving screens all 36 candidates in under half the grid's time and lands within 0.005. Boosting (≈ 0.65) clearly beats Ridge (≈ 0.59).

Think like a practitioner

The best grid score is the maximum of 36 noisy estimates. Is that number an unbiased estimate of future performance? (Next step.)

5Nested cross-validation — how optimistic was that score? 8 min

Why this matters

best_score_ is the best of many tries, so it is optimistically biased — like picking the luckiest of 36 coin-tossers. Nested CV wraps the whole search inside an outer CV: each outer fold tunes on its own inner folds and is scored on data the search never saw. The outer mean is an honest estimate of the whole procedure.

Your tasks

  1. On a 15k sample, run cross_val_score(RandomizedSearchCV(... n_iter=8, cv=3), X, y, cv=outer 3-fold).
  2. Compare the outer mean with the inner best_score_ of the same search fitted on the full sample.
Checkpoint — you should see

Both ≈ 0.63–0.64 and within one standard deviation of each other: with plenty of data and similar candidates the optimism is negligible. That is a finding, not a given — on small data with large grids the inner score can overstate performance badly.

Think like a practitioner

Nested CV gives a score, not a model. Which model do you deploy afterwards?

6Final test, error analysis and selection bias 7 min

Why this matters

Refit the chosen configuration on the full training set and touch the test set once. Then break the error down by segment: a single MAPE hides that some groups (self-employed, the very rich) are much harder.

Your tasks

  1. Set the best random-search params on hgb, fit on all of tr, predict te; report MAPE, R² (log) and the share within ±20%.
  2. MAPE by employment_type and by salary decile (pd.qcut).
Checkpoint — you should see

Test MAPE ≈ 31% (rule 51%), R² ≈ 0.65, ≈ 42% of customers within ±20%. Errors are similar across employment types inside the verified group (only ≈ 900 self-employed to learn from), but much higher in the bottom quintile (≈ 52%) where the model over-estimates — regression to the mean.

Think like a practitioner

Bottom-quintile customers are over-estimated. For an affordability check, is over-estimating income the safe or unsafe direction? Which customers does that put at risk?

7Conservative income for unverified customers 5 min

Why this matters

Credit needs a figure they can defend. Instead of the point estimate, use a conservative one: shift each prediction down by the 20th percentile of the test residuals (log scale), so ≈ 80% of verified customers earn at least that much. Save the model with its residual quantile so the rule is reproducible.

Your tasks

  1. q20 = 20th percentile of log(actual) − log(pred) on test; conservative = estimate × exp(q20).
  2. Score unverified; compare their median estimate with their declared salary; save income_estimates.parquet and income_model.joblib.
Checkpoint — you should see

Coverage 80% by construction; the conservative figure is ≈ 0.74× the estimate. For unverified customers the median declared salary (≈ 12,800) is close to the median estimate (≈ 12,400); the conservative median is ≈ 9,100 AED. Files saved.

Stretch challenges

  1. Replace the P20 shift with a true quantile model: HistGradientBoostingRegressor(loss='quantile', quantile=0.2). Is coverage better per segment?
  2. Use GridSearchCV(..., return_train_score=True) and plot train vs validation R² against max_leaf_nodes — a validation curve from search results.
  3. Weight training rows so self-employed customers count more (sample_weight) — does that reduce their error on test?

Troubleshooting

  • Search too slow → lower the sample to 20k or max_iter to 100.
  • HalvingRandomSearchCV import error → keep the enable_halving_search_cv import before it.
  • Divergent GD for every lr → you forgot to standardise Xg.
