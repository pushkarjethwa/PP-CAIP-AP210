Objective & scenario alignment

Horizon Gulf Bank (HGB) is a fictional UAE retail bank with 250,000 customers. Its Board approved Project MIZAN — an AI decisioning platform covering credit, fraud, collateral valuation, cash logistics, customer retention and document automation. You are a junior ML practitioner in the MIZAN squad.

The Credit Risk team wants an income estimation model for customers whose salary is not paid into HGB (tomorrow's Lab L2.3). Card behaviour is the richest signal the bank holds about those customers, but it lives in 1.5 million transaction rows, not in one row per customer. Your job: build the customer-level transaction feature table, decide which features are worth keeping, and show the KYC team how unstructured images can be described by numbers.

Progressive architecture: joins onto your customers_clean.parquet (L1.1); the feature table feeds income estimation (L2.3) and churn modelling (L3.4, L4.3, L5.3).

By the end of this lab you will be able to

  • Load a large CSV with usecols, category and float32 dtypes and measure the memory saving
  • Aggregate event-level data to one row per customer (RFM, behaviour shares, spend mix, trend)
  • Join safely to a master table (row counts, missing-means-zero vs missing-means-unknown)
  • Select features with variance, correlation pruning and mutual information against a random probe
  • Convert unstructured data (images) into structured features and measure how much they explain

1Load 1.5M rows the memory-smart way 8 min

Why this matters

Pandas stores every repeated string (customer id, merchant category, channel) as a separate Python object. On 1.5M rows that is hundreds of MB. Reading only the columns you need, storing repeated strings as category and amounts as float32 usually cuts memory by 70–90% — the difference between a notebook that runs on a laptop and one that crashes. Saving to Parquet is columnar and compressed, and — unlike CSV — remembers the dtypes, so nobody has to re-specify them.

Your tasks

  1. Read the file naively; record load time and memory_usage(deep=True) in MB; delete it.
  2. Read again with usecols + a dtype map (category for ids and labels, float32 for amount, int8 for the flag) and parse_dates.
  3. Print both timings and memory figures; save to CKPT/card_transactions.parquet, reload it and compare file sizes and dtypes.
Checkpoint — you should see

Naive ≈ 320 MB; smart ≈ 33 MB (≈ 89% less). Parquet is ≈ 19 MB on disk versus 44 MB for the gzipped CSV, and the category/float32 dtypes survive the round-trip. 1,506,430 rows from 58,822 card customers.

Think like a practitioner

Why is customer_id a good category candidate but txn_id is not?

2Aggregate events into one row per customer 12 min

Why this matters

Models need one row per entity (customer), but behaviour arrives as events (transactions). Aggregation is the core feature-engineering move: recency, frequency, monetary (RFM) plus behaviour shares (night, international, e-commerce) and diversity (distinct categories/countries). Confirmed fraud is excluded — it is the fraudster's behaviour, not the customer's. Everything is computed as of 1 Oct 2025 so no future information leaks in.

Your tasks

  1. Drop fraud rows. Add log_amt, is_intl (country ≠ AE), is_night (00–05h), is_ecom (channel E-Commerce).
  2. Group by customer_id (observed=True) with named aggregation: count, total, median, mean/std of log_amt, the three shares, distinct MCCs and countries, first and last timestamp.
  3. Add amt_p90 (90th percentile), recency_days = days from the last transaction to AS_OF, and txn_per_month over the active span.
Checkpoint — you should see

58,798 rows × 13 columns. Median 19 transactions per customer; intl_share ≈ 0.12; median recency_days 6. log_amt_std has ≈ 1,500 NaNs — customers with a single transaction have no spread.

💡 Hint

agg.describe().T is the fastest sanity check: any negative recency or share > 1 means a bug.

3Spend mix and trend features 10 min

Why this matters

Where someone spends says a lot about them: a high share on luxury goods, travel and electronics versus groceries and fuel is a classic affluence signal. A pivot table turns the long category column into one share column per category. A trend feature (last 60 days versus the first 60 days) captures customers who are winding down — useful for churn.

Your tasks

  1. Pivot spend by customer_id × merchant_category (sum, fill 0), divide each row by its total → mix_<category> shares.
  2. Compute spend in the first 60 days (Apr–May) and the last 60 days (Aug–Sep); spend_trend = log((recent+1)/(early+1)).
  3. Join both onto agg and show the five customers with the highest mix_Luxury_Goods — first for everyone, then only for customers with ≥ 10 transactions.
Checkpoint — you should see

58,798 × 28 features; mix rows sum to 1. The first table is all customers with one transaction (share = 100%) — shares computed from tiny counts are noise. With ≥ 10 transactions the top luxury shares are ≈ 50–70%.

Think like a practitioner

spend_trend uses only data before AS_OF. If you computed it using October data, which future model would be leaking? And how would you stop one-transaction customers from producing extreme shares (hint: shrink towards the population average)?

4Join to the customer master — missing means what? 8 min

Why this matters

Only ~59k of 250k customers hold an active card. After a left join the other 190k have no transaction features. For counts and totals missing truly means zero; for averages and shares it means unknown — filling it with 0 would claim these customers spend AED 1 on average. We fill each group correctly and add a has_card_activity flag so a model can tell the two apart.

Your tasks

  1. Left-join feat onto customers_clean[['customer_id']]; assert the row count is unchanged and ids are unique.
  2. Create has_card_activity. Fill counts, totals, mix shares and n_* with 0; fill averages/shares with the card-holder median; set recency_days to 999 for non-users.
  3. Print the share of customers with card activity and confirm there are no NaNs left.
Checkpoint — you should see

≈ 23.5% of customers have card activity; 0 NaNs; 250,000 rows.

Think like a practitioner

A model learns that has_card_activity = 0 customers churn more. Is that a data artefact or a real business signal? How would you check?

5Feature selection with a random probe 12 min

Why this matters

More features ≠ better model. Three cheap filters remove most junk: variance (constant columns carry nothing), correlation pruning (near-duplicates add noise and instability) and mutual information (any — not only linear — dependence on the target). The trick professionals use is a random probe: a column of pure noise. Any feature that scores below the probe is indistinguishable from noise. The target here is the verified salary (salary paid into HGB).

Your tasks

  1. Training population: card-active customers with salary_transfer == 1 and salary_missing == 0; target y = log(monthly_salary_aed).
  2. Add probe_noise (standard normal). Drop zero-variance columns with VarianceThreshold.
  3. Find feature pairs with |Pearson r| > 0.9 and drop the second of each pair.
  4. Compute mutual_info_regression on a 20k sample; keep features scoring above the probe. Plot the ranking.
Checkpoint — you should see

≈ 34.6k training customers. txn_per_month is pruned (near-duplicate of txn_count). 8 of 27 features beat the probe: log_amt_mean, amt_median, amt_p90, spend_total lead, followed by cash-withdrawal share, n_mcc, e-commerce share and money-transfer share. Surprise: luxury and travel shares score at or below noise — the ‘affluence’ intuition from step 3 is not supported by this data. Evidence beats intuition.

Think like a practitioner

Mutual information is computed one feature at a time. Name a case where two individually weak features are strong together.

6Unstructured data → structured features 8 min

Why this matters

Images, text and audio are unstructured: there are no columns. The classical route is to engineer numeric descriptors — ink density, edge strength, where the ink sits on the page — and feed those to a normal model. On Day 4 a CNN will learn its own features from raw pixels; today's numbers are the baseline it has to beat.

Your tasks

  1. Load the 15k KYC images; compute per image: mean ink, ink std, horizontal and vertical edge energy, ink in the top/bottom/left/right halves, number of ‘text rows’ (rows whose ink exceeds the image mean).
  2. Fit a quick multinomial LogisticRegression on 80% / test on 20% and print accuracy against the 20% majority baseline.
Checkpoint — you should see

Hand-made features reach ≈ 67% accuracy — far above the 20% baseline but well below the ≈ 97% the Day 4 CNN achieves by learning its own features.

Think like a practitioner

Which engineered feature would break if the scanner started producing darker images? What does that teach you about monitoring?

Troubleshooting

  • MemoryError on the naive read → skip the naive read; the smart read is what matters.
  • groupby very slow or huge → you forgot observed=True with categorical keys.
  • Merge row count changed → customer_id dtype differs (category vs str); cast the index with .astype(str).
