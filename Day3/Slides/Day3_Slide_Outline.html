2Binary classification and probabilities

  • Model outputs P(y = 1 | x)
  • Threshold converts probabilities into decisions
  • Calibration: does 10% predicted mean 10% observed?
  • Multi-class: one-vs-rest or multinomial (softmax)
🎙 Speaker notes

Real churn scores for 50,000 test customers: churners (gold) score higher on average but the distributions overlap — every threshold trades missed churners against wasted calls. The red line is the top-10% calling budget. Calibration asks whether customers given 0.30 really churn 30% of the time — essential for IFRS 9 PDs. Banks need calibrated PDs for IFRS 9 — not only good ranking.

Key message: A classifier outputs a probability; a threshold turns it into a decision.

Ask the room: Moving the threshold left does what to recall and precision?
Expected: Recall rises (more churners caught), precision falls (more false alarms).

Exam tip: Multi-class: one-vs-rest or softmax (multinomial).

3Logistic regression

  • log-odds = β₀ + βᵀx; p = σ(log-odds)
  • exp(β) = odds ratio
  • Linear decision boundary
  • Regularised by default in scikit-learn (C = 1/λ)
🎙 Speaker notes

Each coefficient adds to the log-odds; exp(β) is the odds ratio. That is the basis of adverse-action reason codes: ‘your bureau score lowered your odds of approval by 40%’. scikit-learn regularises by default (C = 1/λ), so scale features. Odds ratios are the basis of adverse-action reason codes.

Key message: A linear score in log-odds, squeezed into a probability by the sigmoid.

Ask the room: β for ‘num_prior_delinquencies’ is 0.69. Interpretation?
Expected: Each extra delinquency doubles the odds of default (e^0.69 ≈ 2).

Exam tip: Decision boundary is linear in the features; odds ratio = exp(β).

4k-Nearest Neighbours

  • Predict by majority vote / average of the k closest examples
  • Distance metric matters: Euclidean, Manhattan
  • Scale features or the largest unit dominates
  • Lazy learner: no training, expensive prediction; small k overfits
🎙 Speaker notes

Real loan data. Left, unscaled: salary in AED (thousands) swamps bureau score (hundreds), so the boundary is vertical stripes — salary is the only feature that counts. Right, standardised: both features shape the boundary. Small k overfits; large k smooths. Lab 3.1 found k ≈ 200 best for credit. Exam favourite: why does KNN need feature scaling?

Key message: k-NN votes among the closest examples — so the distance must be fair to every feature.

Ask the room: Why is k-NN slow at prediction time?
Expected: It stores all training rows and computes distances to them for every new case (lazy learner).

Exam tip: Always scale features for k-NN, SVM, k-means and neural networks.

▶ Video: K-nearest neighbors, Clearly Explained (StatQuest, 5:30)

5The confusion matrix

  • TP · FP · TN · FN
  • Accuracy = (TP+TN)/all — misleading under imbalance
  • Precision = TP/(TP+FP)
  • Recall (sensitivity, TPR) = TP/(TP+FN)
  • F1 = harmonic mean of precision & recall
🎙 Speaker notes

Real counts: churn model on 50,000 test customers, calling the top 10%. Read across: precision = TP ÷ (TP + FP); recall = TP ÷ (TP + FN). In credit the costly cell is FN (approving a defaulter); in fraud both FN (missed fraud) and FP (a blocked genuine customer) cost money. Draw it. Ask: which cell costs the bank most in credit? in fraud?

Key message: Four cells, and each one has a different cost for the bank.

Ask the room: Compute precision and recall from the matrix.
Expected: Precision = 1,542 ÷ (1,542 + 3,458) ≈ 31%; recall = 1,542 ÷ (1,542 + 4,445) ≈ 26%. Accuracy is 84% — yet calling nobody would score 88%.

Exam tip: Accuracy is misleading under imbalance; F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall.

▶ Video: Machine Learning Fundamentals: The Confusion Matrix (StatQuest, 7:12)

6ROC, AUC and precision–recall

  • ROC: TPR vs FPR over all thresholds; AUC = ranking quality
  • PR curve: better under heavy imbalance
  • Threshold trade-off: more recall ⇒ less precision
  • Choose thresholds from business costs
🎙 Speaker notes

Both curves are the real churn model on test data. ROC-AUC 0.71 means a random churner outranks a random stayer 71% of the time. The PR curve starts high and falls; its baseline is the churn rate (0.12), not 0.5. For fraud at 0.4%, PR-AUC is the honest metric. The PR baseline is the positive rate, not 0.5.

Key message: ROC-AUC measures ranking; PR-AUC tells you how clean the alerts are when positives are rare.

Ask the room: A fraud model has ROC-AUC 0.95 but PR-AUC 0.10. How?
Expected: With very rare positives, a small false-positive rate still means many false alarms per true fraud.

Exam tip: Choose the threshold from business costs, not from 0.5.

▶ Video: ROC and AUC, Clearly Explained! (StatQuest, 16:17)

7Class imbalance

  • Symptoms: high accuracy, useless minority recall
  • Remedies: class weights · resampling (under/over/SMOTE) · threshold moving · cost-sensitive learning
  • Evaluate with PR-AUC, recall@budget, cost
🎙 Speaker notes

Real HGB rates: 12% churn and 0.43% fraud. A model that never flags fraud is over 99% accurate and worthless. Remedies: class weights, resampling (under/over/SMOTE), threshold moving, cost-sensitive learning — and evaluation with PR-AUC, recall at a fixed alert budget, or AED cost. Note: re-weighting/resampling distorts probabilities — recalibrate if PDs are needed.

Key message: With rare positives, accuracy rewards doing nothing.

Ask the room: After training with balanced class weights, the average predicted churn is 0.45. Problem?
Expected: Probabilities are no longer calibrated; ranking is fine but PDs must be recalibrated.

Exam tip: Resampling and re-weighting distort probabilities — recalibrate if you need PDs.

10Clustering

  • Unsupervised: find structure without labels
  • Uses: segmentation, anomaly detection, exploration
  • Quality: inertia, silhouette, business usefulness
🎙 Speaker notes

Real segments from Lab 3.2, projected to 2-D with PCA. Overlap in 2-D is normal; clusters separate in the full feature space. Uses: personas for marketing, peer groups for anomaly detection, exploration. Evaluate with inertia and silhouette, then with the business: can a campaign team act on it? There is no ‘correct’ k — only useful ones.

Key message: Clustering finds structure without labels — judge it by usefulness, not by a single score.

Ask the room: Is there a ‘correct’ number of segments?
Expected: No — choose k by the elbow/silhouette and by actionability and stability.

Exam tip: Unsupervised: no target, no accuracy.

11k-means

  • Choose k; initialise centroids (k-means++)
  • Assign each point to the nearest centroid; recompute centroids; repeat
  • Assumes roughly spherical, similar-size clusters; scale features
  • Choose k: elbow, silhouette, stability, business constraints
🎙 Speaker notes

Three snapshots of the algorithm. k-means assumes roughly spherical, similar-sized clusters, is sensitive to scale and to initialisation (k-means++ fixes most of that). MiniBatchKMeans handles millions of rows. MiniBatchKMeans scales to millions of rows.

Key message: Assign to the nearest centroid, move the centroid, repeat until nothing changes.

Ask the room: Why must you scale features before k-means?
Expected: Distances would be dominated by the largest-unit feature (balance in AED).

Exam tip: Choose k with the elbow (inertia) and silhouette; check stability across seeds.

▶ Video: K-means clustering (StatQuest, 8:30)

12Hierarchical clustering

  • Agglomerative: start with singletons, merge closest pairs
  • Linkage: single, complete, average, Ward
  • Dendrogram: cut at a height to get clusters
  • O(n²) memory — use samples
🎙 Speaker notes

40 real customers with Ward linkage. Long vertical lines mean well-separated clusters; the red cut gives the cluster count. Linkage choice changes shapes: single linkage chains, complete and Ward give compact groups. Memory is O(n²) — use samples, as Lab 3.2 does. Read a dendrogram together: long vertical lines = well-separated clusters.

Key message: Merge the closest groups step by step, then cut the tree where the jumps are large.

Ask the room: Where would you cut this dendrogram and why?
Expected: Where the vertical gap is largest — merging beyond it joins dissimilar groups.

Exam tip: Agglomerative = bottom-up; divisive = top-down.

▶ Video: Hierarchical Clustering (StatQuest, 11:19)

13Decision trees

  • Root → internal nodes (questions) → leaves (predictions)
  • Greedy splits that maximise impurity decrease
  • Gini = 1 − Σp² · Entropy = −Σp log p
  • Classification and regression trees
🎙 Speaker notes

A real depth-2 tree on 80,000 HGB loans. The root splits on AECB score; then prior delinquencies. Each node shows Gini impurity and the class mix. Trees need no scaling, handle interactions and read as rules — that is why credit officers like shallow ones. Trees need no scaling and capture interactions.

Key message: A tree asks the question that best separates the classes, then repeats inside each branch.

Ask the room: Compute Gini for a node with 70% repay, 30% default.
Expected: 1 − (0.7² + 0.3²) = 0.42.

Exam tip: Gini = 1 − Σp²; entropy = −Σp log₂ p; both peak at a 50/50 mix.

▶ Video: Decision and Classification Trees, Clearly Explained!!! (StatQuest, 18:08) — play the first 10 minutes

14Controlling tree complexity

  • max_depth · min_samples_leaf · max_leaf_nodes
  • Cost-complexity pruning (ccp_alpha)
  • Deep trees overfit; shallow trees are interpretable rules
🎙 Speaker notes

Real curve on HGB loans: training AUC keeps rising with depth; validation AUC peaks early, then falls as the tree memorises noise. Controls: max_depth, min_samples_leaf, max_leaf_nodes, and cost-complexity pruning (ccp_alpha). Lab 3.4 shows an unlimited churn tree with train AUC 1.0 and validation 0.53. The lab plots train vs validation PR-AUC by depth.

Key message: Depth is the bias–variance dial of a tree.

Ask the room: Where would you set max_depth from this chart?
Expected: At the validation peak — deeper adds variance without gain.

Exam tip: Pruning removes branches that don’t improve validation performance.

15Random forests & ensembles

  • Bagging: many trees on bootstrap samples, averaged
  • Random feature subsets per split decorrelate trees
  • Lower variance, robust, little tuning
  • Boosting (gradient boosting) instead reduces bias sequentially
🎙 Speaker notes

Random forests are the robust default: little tuning, good accuracy, out-of-bag estimates. Gradient boosting (XGBoost, LightGBM, HistGradientBoosting) usually wins on tabular data but needs tuning and early stopping. The fraud lab uses a forest; the income lab uses histogram gradient boosting. Exam: bagging vs boosting.

Key message: Bagging averages independent trees to reduce variance; boosting adds trees sequentially to reduce bias.

Ask the room: Why does a random forest pick a random subset of features at each split?
Expected: To decorrelate the trees — averaging correlated trees would not reduce variance much.

Exam tip: Bagging ↓ variance (parallel); boosting ↓ bias (sequential).

▶ Video: What is Random Forest? (IBM Technology, 5:21)

16Feature importance

  • Impurity importance: fast, biased to high-cardinality features
  • Permutation importance: drop in validation score when shuffled
  • Correlated features share/split importance
  • Explain to auditors with care
🎙 Speaker notes

Real importances from the Lab 3.3 fraud forest: velocity dominates (transactions in the last hour, seconds since the previous one), followed by a new device, 24-hour counts and spend, and merchant risk. Impurity importance favours continuous, high-cardinality features and splits credit between correlated ones. Permutation importance measures the drop in validation score when one feature is shuffled. Permutation importance is computed on validation data and is model-agnostic — prefer it for audit conversations.

Key message: Impurity importance is fast but biased; permutation importance is the audit-grade answer.

Ask the room: Two features are near-duplicates. What happens to their importance?
Expected: It is shared between them — each looks less important than the information really is.

Exam tip: Permutation importance is model-agnostic and computed on validation data.

20From scores to campaigns

  • Decile table: churn rate and lift per score band
  • Cumulative gains: share of churners reached for x% contacted
  • Expected value = P × success rate × value − cost
  • Budgets turn ranking into a decision
🎙 Speaker notes

Real churn deciles on test data: decile 1 churns at about 2.6× the base rate. The gains curve says how many churners you reach by calling x% of customers. Lab 3.4 showed that targeting by expected value (probability × success rate × customer value − cost) more than doubles campaign value versus targeting the highest probabilities. Lift is the language retention and collections teams speak.

Key message: Rank customers, cut by budget, and target by expected value — not by probability alone.

Ask the room: Who is worth more to call: P = 0.40 with AED 2k value, or P = 0.20 with AED 20k value?
Expected: EV ∝ 0.40×2k = 800 vs 0.20×20k = 4,000 — the second customer.

Exam tip: Lift = response rate in a segment ÷ overall rate.

21Trees for regression — and their limits

  • Leaf predicts the mean of its training rows
  • Piecewise constant: cannot extrapolate beyond the training range
  • Fix trends with a linear base or relative targets
  • Algorithm choice depends on the data, not on fashion
🎙 Speaker notes

Synthetic illustration of what Lab 3.4 found on real property data: the random forest flattens out in the future while the linear model follows the trend. On the 2025 out-of-time AVM test, the Day 2 linear model (9.75% MAPE) beat the forest (10.8%). Fix trends with a linear base, de-trended or relative targets. Today a logistic regression beats the forest on churn — discuss why.

Key message: A tree predicts averages of past rows — it cannot go beyond the range it has seen.

Ask the room: Today a logistic regression beat the forest on churn. Does that surprise you?
Expected: Not on noisy, mostly additive tabular data — algorithm choice depends on the data, not fashion.

Exam tip: Regression tree leaf = mean of its training targets → piecewise constant.
