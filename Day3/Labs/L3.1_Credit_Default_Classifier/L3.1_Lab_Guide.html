Objective & scenario alignment

Horizon Gulf Bank (HGB) is a fictional UAE retail bank with 250,000 customers. Its Board approved Project MIZAN — an AI decisioning platform covering credit, fraud, collateral valuation, cash logistics, customer retention and document automation. You are a junior ML practitioner in the MIZAN squad.

The Retail Credit Committee meets on Thursday. They need: a PD model better than the L1.2 baseline, evidence that its probabilities are calibrated (they feed IFRS 9 provisions), a recommended approval cut-off with its AED impact, and a five-grade risk scale for pricing.

Progressive architecture: consumes L1.2’s artefacts; its saved policy is registered on Day 5.

By the end of this lab you will be able to

  • Fit and interpret logistic regression via odds ratios
  • Show why k-NN needs feature scaling, and tune k
  • Compare unweighted vs class-weighted models on PR-AUC and calibration
  • Optimise the threshold on a per-loan cost matrix, with an approval-rate constraint
  • Train and evaluate a multi-class risk-grade model (macro vs weighted F1)
  • Evaluate once on the out-of-time test set and persist the policy

2Logistic regression and odds ratios 12 min

Why this matters

A credit committee wants to know why the model scores someone high. Logistic coefficients are log-odds; exp(coef) is the odds ratio per one-standard-deviation change of a scaled feature — directly explainable and the basis of adverse-action reasons.

Your tasks

  1. Fit Pipeline(prep=clone(preprocessor), clf=LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000)) on train.
  2. Write scores(y, p) → ROC-AUC, PR-AUC (average_precision_score), Brier score.
  3. Show the 8 features with the largest and 5 with the smallest odds ratios.
Checkpoint — you should see

ROC-AUC ≈ 0.83, PR-AUC ≈ 0.33. DBR (≈ 1.6×), prior delinquencies (≈ 1.5×), weaker employer category and missing bureau file raise the odds; a higher bureau score (≈ 0.45×) and Government employment lower them.

🔑 Reference solution (Level 3 code)
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score, average_precision_score, brier_score_loss
y_tr, y_va = train[TARGET].to_numpy(), valid[TARGET].to_numpy()

def scores(y, p) -> dict:
    return {"ROC_AUC": roc_auc_score(y, p), "PR_AUC": average_precision_score(y, p), "Brier": brier_score_loss(y, p)}

logit = Pipeline([("prep", clone(preprocessor)), ("clf", LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000))]).fit(train, y_tr)
p_logit = logit.predict_proba(valid)[:, 1]
board = {"logistic": scores(y_va, p_logit)}
odds = pd.Series(np.exp(logit.named_steps["clf"].coef_[0]), index=logit.named_steps["prep"].get_feature_names_out())
print("raise default odds:\n", odds.sort_values(ascending=False).head(8).round(2))
print("lower default odds:\n", odds.sort_values().head(5).round(2))
pd.DataFrame(board).T.round(4)
Think like a practitioner

An odds ratio of 1.6 on num_prior_delinquencies_24m — explain it to a branch manager in one sentence.

3k-NN: the scaling experiment and tuning k 15 min

Why this matters

k-NN classifies by majority vote of the nearest neighbours — distance on raw AED salaries swamps every other feature. Scaling is not optional. k-NN is also a lazy learner: no training cost, but prediction cost grows with data, so we use samples.

Your tasks

  1. Take a 40k training sample and 10k validation sample. Using 6 raw numeric columns (median-imputed), compare k-NN (k=51) without and with StandardScaler.
  2. With the full preprocessor, tune k ∈ {5, 15, 51, 101, 201} by validation ROC-AUC; plot it.
  3. Time predict_proba for the best k and add it to board.
Checkpoint — you should see

Unscaled ≈ 0.60–0.65 vs scaled ≈ 0.75+. AUC rises with k up to ~100–200. k-NN trails logistic and is much slower to score.

💡 Hint

Distance-based models: the feature with the largest numeric range dominates Euclidean distance.

🔑 Reference solution (Level 3 code)
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.impute import SimpleImputer
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
tr_s = train.sample(40_000, random_state=RNG); va_s = valid.sample(10_000, random_state=RNG)
RAW = ["monthly_salary_aed", "loan_amount_aed", "aecb_score", "debt_burden_ratio", "num_prior_delinquencies_24m", "num_bureau_inquiries_6m"]
for name, steps in [("unscaled", [SimpleImputer(strategy="median")]), ("scaled", [SimpleImputer(strategy="median"), StandardScaler()])]:
    knn = Pipeline([(f"s{i}", s) for i, s in enumerate(steps)] + [("knn", KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=51))]).fit(tr_s[RAW], tr_s[TARGET])
    print(f"k-NN {name:8s} AUC = {roc_auc_score(va_s[TARGET], knn.predict_proba(va_s[RAW])[:, 1]):.3f}")
ks, aucs = [5, 15, 51, 101, 201], []
for k in ks:
    m = Pipeline([("prep", clone(preprocessor)), ("knn", KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=k))]).fit(tr_s, tr_s[TARGET])
    aucs.append(roc_auc_score(va_s[TARGET], m.predict_proba(va_s)[:, 1]))
plt.plot(ks, aucs, "o-"); plt.xscale("log"); plt.xlabel("k"); plt.ylabel("validation AUC"); plt.title("k-NN: small k overfits, large k smooths"); plt.show()
best_k = ks[int(np.argmax(aucs))]
knn = Pipeline([("prep", clone(preprocessor)), ("knn", KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=best_k))]).fit(tr_s, tr_s[TARGET])
t0 = time.perf_counter(); p_knn = knn.predict_proba(valid)[:, 1]; dt = time.perf_counter() - t0
board[f"kNN k={best_k} (40k sample)"] = scores(y_va, p_knn)
print(f"best k={best_k}; scoring {len(valid):,} loans took {dt:.1f}s"); pd.DataFrame(board).T.round(4)
Think like a practitioner

When would k-NN genuinely be the right tool in a bank? (case-based reasoning: show the 10 most similar past applicants to an underwriter)

4Imbalance: class weights vs calibration 10 min

Why this matters

With ~7% defaulters the model can be lazy about the minority class. class_weight='balanced' forces attention to defaulters — but distorts the probabilities. A reliability (calibration) curve shows whether a predicted 10% PD really defaults 10% of the time.

Your tasks

  1. Fit a class_weight='balanced' logistic model; add its scores to board.
  2. Plot calibration curves (calibration_curve, 10 quantile bins) for both models against the diagonal.
  3. Decide which model is fit for IFRS 9 PDs and write it down.
Checkpoint — you should see

Same ROC-AUC; the unweighted model hugs the diagonal (low Brier) while the balanced model sits far below it — over-predicting PD.

🔑 Reference solution (Level 3 code)
from sklearn.calibration import calibration_curve
logit_bal = Pipeline([("prep", clone(preprocessor)), ("clf", LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000, class_weight="balanced"))]).fit(train, y_tr)
p_bal = logit_bal.predict_proba(valid)[:, 1]
board["logistic balanced"] = scores(y_va, p_bal)
for name, pp in [("logistic", p_logit), ("balanced", p_bal)]:
    frac_pos, mean_pred = calibration_curve(y_va, pp, n_bins=10, strategy="quantile")
    plt.plot(mean_pred, frac_pos, "o-", label=name)
plt.plot([0, .6], [0, .6], "k--", label="perfect"); plt.xlabel("mean predicted PD"); plt.ylabel("observed default rate"); plt.legend(); plt.show()
pd.DataFrame(board).T.round(4)

5Cost-optimal cut-off with an approval-rate constraint 13 min

Why this matters

Credit policy is a business decision. Using per-loan economics — a missed default costs its actual loss; a wrongly rejected good loan costs forgone margin — we find the cost-minimising PD cut-off, then check it against the commercial constraint of ≥ 80% approvals.

Your tasks

  1. Write policy_cost(p, df, t) returning total cost in AED for threshold t (reject if PD ≥ t).
  2. Sweep t ∈ [0.01, 0.5]; plot cost and approval rate on twin axes.
  3. Find t_cost (unconstrained) and t_policy (cheapest t with approval ≥ 80%). Report precision, recall, F1 at each.
Checkpoint — you should see

Cost-optimal t ≈ 0.24 with ≈ 92% approvals, so the ≥ 80% constraint is not binding here (both rows identical). The naive 0.5 cut-off costs ≈ AED 15m more and catches only ≈ 12% of defaulters vs ≈ 39%.

🔑 Reference solution (Level 3 code)
from sklearn.metrics import precision_score, recall_score, f1_score
def policy_cost(p, d, t) -> float:
    reject = p >= t
    fn = (~reject) & (d[TARGET].to_numpy() == 1)              # approved defaulter
    fp = reject & (d[TARGET].to_numpy() == 0)                  # rejected good borrower
    margin = 0.03 * d.loan_amount_aed.to_numpy() * np.maximum(d.tenure_months.to_numpy(), 12) / 12
    return d.loss_amount_aed.to_numpy()[fn].sum() + margin[fp].sum()

ths = np.round(np.arange(0.01, 0.51, 0.01), 2)
curve = pd.DataFrame({"t": ths, "cost": [policy_cost(p_logit, valid, t) for t in ths], "approval": [(p_logit < t).mean() for t in ths]})
t_cost = curve.loc[curve.cost.idxmin(), "t"]
t_policy = curve[curve.approval >= 0.80].sort_values("cost").iloc[0].t
fig, ax = plt.subplots(); ax.plot(curve.t, curve.cost / 1e6, label="cost (AED m)"); ax2 = ax.twinx(); ax2.plot(curve.t, curve.approval, "g--", label="approval rate")
ax.axvline(t_cost, color="r", ls=":"); ax.axvline(t_policy, color="purple", ls="-."); ax.set_xlabel("PD cut-off"); ax.set_ylabel("AED m"); ax2.set_ylabel("approval"); plt.show()
for name, t in [("cost-optimal", t_cost), ("policy (≥80% approve)", t_policy), ("naive 0.5", 0.5)]:
    yhat = (p_logit >= t).astype(int)
    print(f"{name:22s} t={t:.2f} approve={(1 - yhat.mean()):.1%} cost=AED {policy_cost(p_logit, valid, t) / 1e6:6.1f}m "
          f"precision={precision_score(y_va, yhat):.2f} recall={recall_score(y_va, yhat):.2f} F1={f1_score(y_va, yhat):.2f}")

6Multi-class risk grade (A–E) 12 min

Why this matters

Pricing uses grades, not a binary flag. A multinomial logistic model predicts five ordered classes. With imbalanced classes, macro-F1 (every grade equal) and weighted-F1 (by frequency) tell different stories.

Your tasks

  1. Train LogisticRegression(max_iter=3000) on risk_grade using a 80k training sample and the cloned preprocessor.
  2. Print the classification report and plot the 5×5 confusion matrix (normalised by true class).
  3. Compute the share of errors that are one grade away (adjacent).
Checkpoint — you should see

Accuracy ≈ 67% (macro-F1 ≈ 0.59 < weighted-F1 ≈ 0.67), but ≈ 97% of predictions are within one grade; middle grades C–D are hardest.

🔑 Reference solution (Level 3 code)
from sklearn.metrics import classification_report, ConfusionMatrixDisplay
tr_g = train.sample(80_000, random_state=RNG)
grader = Pipeline([("prep", clone(preprocessor)), ("clf", LogisticRegression(max_iter=3000))]).fit(tr_g, tr_g.risk_grade)
g_pred = grader.predict(valid)
print(classification_report(valid.risk_grade, g_pred, digits=3))
ConfusionMatrixDisplay.from_predictions(valid.risk_grade, g_pred, normalize="true", cmap="Purples", values_format=".2f"); plt.show()
dist = np.abs(valid.risk_grade.map("ABCDE".index).to_numpy() - pd.Series(g_pred).map("ABCDE".index).to_numpy())
print(f"exact: {(dist == 0).mean():.1%} | within one grade: {(dist <= 1).mean():.1%}")

Troubleshooting
